# Room names hinge on who orders claims

No design ticks all ten boxes, and the reason is a theorem, not a gap in the survey: first-come uniqueness of a human-chosen name cannot be enforced among free identities without either an orderer or a Sybil countermeasure ([Kleppmann & Howard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00472)). Everything else on the wishlist (owner-only updates, renewal, release, persistence while the owner is away, PoW gating, 100k names with local lookups) is achievable with self-certifying signed records parked on public Nostr relays, and is the same under every option. The options therefore differ in one thing only: who decides which of two first claims on a free name wins, and who decides an expiry race. The notes support seven candidates: an ALFIS-like signer-quorum chain, a witnessed append-only log named in the room link, relay-parked claims bracketed in time by drand and OpenTimestamps with a settling period, a clock-free heaviest-work rule, a registrar key (single or k-of-n) in the link, GNS-style petnames with no room-wide uniqueness, and a tiered hybrid. The witnessed log is the only one that delivers hard first-come, but it needs an always-on server that this project does not have; the time-bracketed design needs no server and no key but leaves names provisional and open to pre-stamped, withheld claims priced only by proof of work. My recommendation is the tiered hybrid: build the shared layer and petnames first, make time-bracketed claims the default arbiter for ownerless rooms with names shown as provisional until settled, and let a room's link name k-of-n cosigners when it wants hard finality, in the same way a serious room already names its own relays and derper. Before any spec is written, four things need measuring: relay retention over weeks, browser-versus-native PoW cost, signature-verification time for a 100k-record bootstrap in WASM, and OpenTimestamps time-to-anchor from a page.

## First-come uniqueness cannot exist without an orderer

The framing result is Kleppmann and Howard's: a peer-to-peer database can be immune to Sybil attacks "if and only if all of the possible transactions are I-confluent" with respect to the application's invariants, and uniqueness of a user-chosen attribute is their first counter-example, since two inserts that are each valid alone violate the constraint together. Their table lists uniqueness as unsafe when the value is user-chosen and safe only "if the value is determined by the hash of the message containing the update" ([arXiv:2012.00472](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00472)). That loophole is exactly the names rooms have today, `<key>.<tag>.p2p.internal`, which need no agreement because the name is the key ([rooms.md](../docs/networking/rooms.md)). Human-chosen names are the unsafe case by definition.

The consensus-number result sharpens where the difficulty sits. An asset with one owner has consensus number 1 and needs no consensus; an object that k parties may claim has consensus number k ([Guerraoui et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05574)). The notes map this onto a registry as follows (their inference): once a name is claimed, every later operation is single-writer (update, renewal inside the owner's window, release, transfer) and a per-name signed chain handles it with any number of Sybils present. Agreement is needed for exactly two events: a claim on a free name, and the moment a name passes from "owned, in its renewal window" to "free", which is a claim-versus-renewal race and also needs a shared notion of time.

Three consequences follow, and they bound every option below. First, **a rule that converges without an orderer does not define "first"**; it defines something else (most work, lowest hash, earliest anchored timestamp), and each such rule is a theft rule for whoever can game it. Second, **time alone is not order**: OpenTimestamps' own author states that a timestamp "only proves a message existed" and that "timestamps do not by themselves solve the doublespend problem" ([Todd](https://petertodd.org/2016/opentimestamps-announcement)); a claim can be stamped, withheld and revealed later. Third, no protocol has both liveness under dynamic participation and safety under partition ([arXiv:2302.11326](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.11326)), so a design either lets registration proceed while members are partitioned or alone and treats names as provisional, or it refuses registration when its orderer is unreachable. Rooms make the first case common: the usual "partition" is two members online at disjoint times, each alone when it registers.

The wishlist is therefore over-constrained. Unpermissioned (box 2), no re-registration before expiry (box 4), no always-online dependency (box 8) and no master key (box 10) cannot all hold at full strength. The deployed systems confirm it: every surveyed naming system uses a coin, a trusted orderer, or gives up global first-come ([Tor's onion-names survey](https://onionservices.torproject.org/research/proposals/usability/discovery/onion-names/), [did:plc](https://web.plc.directory/spec/v0.1/did-plc), [RFC 9498](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9498.html)). The one coinless chain, ALFIS, met the boxes together by growing a small signer set, discussed under option 1.

## Nine-tenths of the system is identical under every option

The owner's choice is narrower than it looks, because the record format, storage, sync, lookup and anti-spam layers do not depend on it. This section fixes that shared layer so the options can be compared on the single part that varies. Sizes and CPU figures here are the researchers' own arithmetic unless a source is given, and none has been measured in this codebase.

### The record and its per-name chain

A record binds a name to a node key, with an expiry, a sequence number and a signature. Because a room address is the room's /48 plus the first 80 bits of the node key, and because a lookup can be sealed to its target only with the full key, the record should carry the 32-byte node key and let the resolver compute the single AAAA from it, as it already does for key names ([rooms.md](../docs/networking/rooms.md)). The notes estimate **about 47 bytes unsigned, 111 bytes with one 64-byte signature and 143 bytes when a separate owner key signs for the node key**; wrapped as a Nostr event the same record is roughly 300–650 bytes. At 100k names that is an estimated 11–14 MB in binary and 30–65 MB per relay as events. Compression saves at most a few percent because keys and signatures are random.

Each name gets a hash-chained log of operations (claim, update, renew, release), each signed by the current owner key and naming its predecessor's hash. This is did:plc's shape, in production at Bluesky, where the directory "can only deny service or misorder forks", not forge ([did:plc spec](https://web.plc.directory/spec/v0.1/did-plc)), and Nano's, where each account chain has one writer and nodes can prune to the head block ([Nano docs](https://docs.nano.org/protocol-design/ledger/)). Two successors of one entry signed by the same key are a compact, transferable proof of equivocation. This chain alone delivers box 3 in full: only the registering identity can manage or release, and it can renew at any point before expiry. did:plc's 72-hour override by a higher-priority recovery key is an optional pattern for key loss ([did:plc spec](https://web.plc.directory/spec/v0.1/did-plc)).

**Room keys cannot sign.** Node keys are X25519, and rooms.md states plainly that "an X25519 key can't sign". Two routes exist: a separate BIP-340 owner key, which costs 32 bytes per record but uses `k256`, already a dependency for Nostr signing; or XEdDSA over the node key, which the researchers cite from memory of [Signal's specification](https://signal.org/docs/specifications/xeddsa/) and did not re-read. A separate owner key has a side benefit: the name survives a change of node key, and if the record is a Nostr addressable event signed by the owner key, the event's own signature is the record's signature and relays enforce latest-wins per (kind, pubkey, `d`) for free ([NIP-01](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/01.md)). It has a cost too: rooms today sign Nostr events with a key that is random per session so relays cannot link a node's sessions; an owner key is persistent by nature.

### Hashed-name commitment, lifecycle and proof of work

ALFIS stores `identity = SHA256(SHA256(name))` and `confirmation = SHA256(SHA256(name) || signing_key)`, so a watcher cannot re-register a pending name under its own key without knowing the name ([transaction.rs](https://github.com/Revertron/Alfis/blob/master/src/blockchain/transaction.rs)). That is free and worth copying, with one caveat that is my own reasoning: a dictionary word is recoverable from an unsalted hash, so against an orderer who can insert its own entry first, a salted commit followed by a reveal, as Namecoin does with a 12-block minimum delay ([Namecoin FAQ](https://www.namecoin.org/docs/faq/)), is the stronger form. drand timelock encryption can automate the reveal ([drand](https://docs.drand.love/blog/2023/03/28/timelock-on-fastnet)).

Lifecycle parameters have production precedents to choose from: ALFIS's 365-day lifetime, 30-day owner-only grace, one new name per key per day and discounted renewals ([constants.rs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Revertron/Alfis/master/src/commons/constants.rs)); Namecoin's semi-expiry, where a name stops resolving about 28 days before ownership lapses ([Namecoin FAQ](https://www.namecoin.org/docs/faq/)); and ENS's 90-day grace followed by a 21-day decaying premium that removes the race at the expiry instant ([ENS support](https://support.ens.domains/en/articles/12311757-what-happens-when-an-ens-name-expires)). The coinless translation of the last is PoW difficulty for a just-expired name that starts high and decays.

Proof of work is sound as a fee and useless as security. A browser ran the same SHA-256 work about 1.7–2x slower than native in mCaptcha's measurement ([mCaptcha](https://mcaptcha.org/blog/pow-performance/)), and an old argon2-browser table shows a 5–8x gap for Argon2d at 1 MiB, which the notes think overstates today's gap ([argon2-browser](https://github.com/antelle/argon2-browser)). From those, with cloud prices the researchers assumed rather than sourced, they estimate that one laptop-minute of memory-hard PoW per name costs an attacker roughly **$2–30 per 10,000 names**, and that plain SHA-256 PoW is a speed bump only. Treat those dollar figures as unverified estimates. The levers that change the slope are recurring cost on renewal, work bound to a recent beacon so it cannot be stockpiled, a committed target as in [NIP-13](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/13.md), and a per-key rate limit that only bites if keys themselves cost work, as ALFIS's mined keys do. ALFIS's own RFCs warn that discounted, unthrottled renewals become a cheap way to fake activity ([RFC-0002](https://github.com/Revertron/Alfis/blob/master/docs/rfc/0002-healing-signatures.md)). Kalodner et al. found Namecoin's flat fee left about 28 of roughly 120,000 names in real use ([WEIS 2015](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~arvindn/publications/namespaces.pdf)); the notes argue a room-scoped namespace has no market and so needs squatting to be annoying rather than impossible, which is an argument, not a measurement.

### Storage, sync and lookup

Every member keeps the whole registry. DHTs are a poor fit: the state is smaller than what a DHT would save, every hop to a new browser peer costs a cold lookup of about 0.3–1.4 s on public servers ([bench/net/REPORT.md](../bench/net/REPORT.md)), and a DHT holds nothing when nobody is online. With a local replica a lookup is a hash-map hit, and the 2-second target matters only for a newcomer still syncing and for names registered seconds ago. A newcomer can resolve one name before it has synced by asking the link's relays for that name's tag in a single REQ, since relays index single-letter tags ([NIP-01](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/01.md)).

Public Nostr relays are the only open-write, fee-free, account-free, multi-operator store a page can reach. In the researchers' probe of 2026-11-15, 13 of 15 relays stored unknown regular and addressable kinds from fresh keys with no AUTH, PoW or payment, including all six of the rooms' default relays; 8 of 13 answered NIP-77 Negentropy, among them four of the six defaults (relay.novospes.com, relay.routstr.com, nostr.rblb.it, relay.nostrmap.net). The same probe saw per-key write limits of 30 and 60 events a minute and one relay that cut a fresh key off after 15 events. That is one day from one IP, not a policy. strfry's defaults allow 64 KiB events and Negentropy sessions up to a million events ([strfry.conf](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hoytech/strfry/master/strfry.conf)). **No relay publishes a retention commitment, and no study of pruning was found**; a 2023 measurement found about half of relays up more than 99% of the time and a fifth down more than 40% ([Wei & Tyson](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05709v2)). Box 5 therefore rests on writing to several relays and on any member re-publishing what a relay has lost, which works because signed records can be re-sent by anyone without the private key, as BEP44 items can ([BEP 44](https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0044.html)).

Range-based set reconciliation is the repair primitive. Negentropy needs no parameter guess, takes 2–3 round trips at 100k items by the notes' calculation from its branching factor, and has stable JavaScript and Rust implementations ([hoytech/negentropy](https://github.com/hoytech/negentropy), [Hoyte](https://logperiodic.com/rbsr.html)). Sorting by expiry instead of creation time turns expired records into a prefix both sides skip, so no tombstones are needed (the notes' design inference). Live updates can ride either a shared relay tag that every member subscribes to, or member-to-member gossip in the HyParView and Plumtree style; the researchers cite those two protocols from memory and estimate steady-state gossip at 10–15 bytes a second per node. A returning node loads its own OPFS or IndexedDB copy and reconciles the difference; Chromium lets an origin use up to 60% of the disk ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria)), so storage is not a constraint beside disk images.

The cost that matters is CPU at first sync. The notes assume 150–400 µs per signature check in WASM, giving **15–40 s of one core for 100k records**; that is an assumption to be measured, and it argues for verifying in a worker and lazily. A room member's module costs about 1.4 MiB today ([bench/net/REPORT.md](../bench/net/REPORT.md)); a 100k replica adds tens of MB.

### Time, and what the shared layer changes in rooms

Expiry needs a clock no member controls. drand's quicknet emits a threshold-BLS beacon every 3 seconds, each round verifiable alone, from two CORS-open hosts ([drand](https://docs.drand.love/blog/2023/10/16/quicknet-is-live), researchers' probe); a record embedding round R provably did not exist before R. Verifying it needs a BLS12-381 pairing library, a new dependency whose WASM cost is unmeasured. Bitcoin block headers fetched from explorers are a coarser, PoW-checkable alternative that needs only SHA-256. Nostr's `created_at` is useless for this: all 13 accepting relays stored an event dated a year in the past.

Four binding rooms decisions are touched by any option, and changing them is the owner's call. Rooms today store nothing on relays ("nothing about a node stays on a relay"); a registry stores events there. Rooms have "no directory in the protocol"; a registry is one. A shared registry tag or a gossip overlay reveals that an address is online, against "nothing lists who is in it"; syncing only against relays avoids member-to-member discovery at the cost of leaning harder on relays. And the resolver at `<prefix>::53`, which today answers NXDOMAIN for anything that is not a key name, would answer registered names, a guest-visible change. The rooms data channel is unordered and unreliable, so bulk sync between members needs a second, reliable channel. Records in a private room can be sealed and tagged with keys derived from the room ID, as lookups are, so relays learn neither names nor the room; in a public room every reader is a member and PoW is the only gate.

## Seven architectures, one difference: who orders first claims

Each option below assumes the shared layer and states only what it adds. The order runs from the design closest to a blockchain to the one with no agreement at all, then the hybrid.

### Option 1: a signer-quorum chain in the ALFIS mould

ALFIS is the closest existing system to the wishlist and it is a working, maintained one: version 0.10.0, last commits in November 2026, 1,391 domains ever registered and 594 resolving in July 2026 ([Alfis](https://github.com/Revertron/Alfis), [RFC-0002](https://github.com/Revertron/Alfis/blob/master/docs/rfc/0002-healing-signatures.md)). Its lesson is about where its ordering really comes from. PoW there is a fixed per-domain fee (24 bits, never adjusted) and a mined-key requirement; order and finality come from signatures: each domain block must be locked by 4 of 7 signer keys drawn from past block authors, forks deeper than 4 blocks are rejected, and the binary carries hard-coded checkpoints ([constants.rs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Revertron/Alfis/master/src/commons/constants.rs), [chain.rs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Revertron/Alfis/master/src/blockchain/chain.rs)). The project's own RFCs record that only 16 keys, held by five or six people, pass the eligibility threshold, and that the chain **stalled on 2026-07-05** when offline signer keys left a block one signature short. It was patched: "healing" lets a standby pool supply missing signatures after a timeout, and proven-dead keys are now excluded from the draw ([RFC-0002](https://github.com/Revertron/Alfis/blob/master/docs/rfc/0002-healing-signatures.md), [RFC-0003](https://github.com/Revertron/Alfis/blob/master/docs/rfc/0003-dead-key-exclusion.md)). So ALFIS's ordering rests on a small signer set and on its author's releases and checkpoints.

**How a room version works.** Each room has its own chain. To register, a member mines a domain block (hashed name, owner key, node key, expiry) and publishes it to the link's relays as a stored event; seven signers are drawn from the chain's history and four must add light signing blocks before the next domain block is accepted. Updates and renewals are further blocks by the owner key; a name expires after its lifetime plus an owner-only grace and is then free. Lookup is a local index over the chain; sync is fetching blocks from relays or peers and verifying them. When the room is empty the chain sits on relays and nothing moves.

**What breaks in a room.** The signer set is the problem transplanted. ALFIS's signers are home servers and still decayed; a room's would be browser tabs. A registration waits until four drawn signers are next online, which in a quiet room is days or never, and ALFIS showed that a stall strands names whose grace elapses meanwhile ([RFC-0002](https://github.com/Revertron/Alfis/blob/master/docs/rfc/0002-healing-signatures.md)). In an open room the earliest or busiest registrants become the committee, and a critic of ALFIS showed that eligibility drawn from ledger activity is buyable with modest compute ([issue #364](https://github.com/Revertron/Alfis/issues/364)). ALFIS's remedy for deep forks is a checkpoint shipped by the author; a per-room chain has nobody to ship one, so a newcomer shown an alternative history by an attacker cannot tell, the attack the same critic described. A plain heaviest-chain rule is worse: with no coin there is no honest hashpower floor, and a rented-hash attack rewrote Ethereum Classic three times in August 2020 ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/41492/rented-hash-power-huge-problem-for-proof-of-work-etc-labs-ceo)).

**Boxes, cost, risk.** It ticks 1, 3, 5, 6, 7 and 9; box 2 partly (unpermissioned to register, but a de facto committee); box 4 only while the signer quorum is honest and awake; box 8 partly (no server, but a quorum of members must come online); box 10 formally. Browser cost is light to verify; mining competes with the emulator. Squatting is priced by PoW; front-running is blunted by the hashed name; partitions fork and one side's names vanish on merge. It reuses relay parking and adds a chain, a signer draw and fork handling. Maturity is the paradox: the only deployed coinless name chain, but with no browser node and no WASM build, so a port is new work whose central mechanism the upstream project is still repairing. **Measure first:** how many long-lived members real rooms have, since that decides whether a quorum ever assembles.

### Option 2: a witnessed append-only log named in the room link

This is the plan page's own sketch ([networking-p2p.md](../docs/plans/networking-p2p.md)) and the consensus researchers' "best fit found". The link names a log (URL and key) and n witness keys with a threshold k, alongside its relays and DERP regions.

**How it works.** To register, the page computes the PoW and submits a salted commitment to the log over HTTPS; the log appends it and publishes a checkpoint once k witnesses have cosigned. A witness keeps only the last checkpoint per log and cosigns a new one only against a valid consistency proof from the size it last saw ([C2SP tlog-witness](https://github.com/C2SP/C2SP/blob/main/tlog-witness.md)), so with an honest quorum the log cannot show two histories. The claimant then reveals the name; the log's sequence is the order, and the earliest commitment wins. The record plus its inclusion proof and the cosigned checkpoint is parked on relays and gossiped like any other. Updates and renewals travel on the per-name chain; expiry and re-registration are log entries, with a drand round in each checkpoint as the clock. Lookups are local; a newcomer can check a record, or a name's absence, against a cosigned root with a proof the notes estimate at about 550 bytes for 100k names. When the room is empty the log is still there and nothing is lost. If the log itself disappears, existing names keep resolving from replicas and parked checkpoints, and new registrations stop.

**Attacks and limits.** The log operator can censor, delay, or insert its own entry ahead; commit-reveal handles the last. Rewriting history takes the log key plus k witness keys, and anchoring checkpoints with OpenTimestamps leaves an audit trail even then. Witnesses check consistency, not content, so every client re-validates signatures and expiry, which is cheap at this size. A Sigsum maintainer's caveat applies: a cosignature says no split view "has been presented to the witness", not that none exists ([sigsum-general](https://lists.sigsum.org/mailman3/hyperkitty/list/sigsum-general@lists.sigsum.org/message/R7PREM72YFEN26O35WN24WMGUVVY3MTF)). Sybils and partitions are irrelevant: there is one order, and when it is unreachable registration is refused rather than forked, which is what CT, did:plc and DNS registries all do.

**The obstacle is who runs it.** A log is a server, and the project has no backend. The persistence researchers found nothing usable from a page as it stands: Sigsum's public logs rate-limit by DNS domain and store only checksums, a Sigsum log endpoint they probed sent no CORS header, and the transparency.dev witness network cosigns logs that an operator registers, with no evidence either way that it would take one small log per room ([transparency.dev](https://blog.transparency.dev/can-i-get-a-witness-network)). So this option exists only for rooms whose creator runs a log and finds witnesses, and the global room, a bare ID with no link parameters, has none unless the page ships defaults, which makes the page's author the authority. There is also unresearched design work: witnesses cosign append-only trees, not a mutable map, so pruning expired names under cosigned roots needs a map-over-log construction or periodic cosigned snapshots.

**Boxes, cost, risk.** It ticks 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 9 in full; box 2 partly (anyone registers, a named party can censor); box 8 fails; box 10 partly, since the link carries keys, though k-of-n keys that can censor but not forge are a weaker power than a master key. Browser cost is a few Merkle and signature checks. The parts are the most mature available (CT since 2013, Sigsum v1, C2SP specs); no deployed system assembles them into browser-only naming. **Prototype first:** a room-scale log plus two witnesses behind CORS, and an answer from the witness network on small logs.

### Option 3: relay-parked claims bracketed in time, with a settling period

This is the strongest design that needs no server of anyone's and no key in the link. Order is replaced by externally anchored time.

**How it works.** A claim embeds a recent drand round, which proves it was made after that moment, and carries PoW bound to that round. The claimant publishes it to the link's relays and submits its hash to OpenTimestamps calendars, which answered browsers with open CORS in the probe and return a pending receipt in about a second; the Bitcoin-anchored proof arrives hours later and bounds the claim's existence to before a block ([Todd](https://petertodd.org/2016/opentimestamps-announcement)). Some member, the owner or anyone, later upgrades the proof and re-publishes it. The rule is deterministic over whatever claims a node holds: among valid claims for a free name, **the one anchored in the earliest Bitcoin block wins, ties broken by lowest claim hash**; a claim whose bracket (drand round to anchor block) is wider than a set bound is invalid, which forces prompt stamping. Every node with the same claims computes the same owner, so partitions merge without negotiation. Renewal is a new bracketed entry on the per-name chain anchored before expiry plus grace; a stranger's claim on an expired name is valid only if its drand round falls after the grace ends. The page shows a name as provisional until its anchor is some days old and no earlier claim has surfaced. In an empty room nothing is needed: claims and proofs sit on relays, and a lone visitor can register.

**The withheld claim is the residual hole, and it cannot be closed without witnesses.** The notes walk through it: an attacker builds claims for a dictionary of names, stamps one Merkle root, publishes nothing, and reveals an earlier-bracketed claim after an honest user registers. A bracket proves when a claim existed, never that it was public. Every patch that enforces "published within W" needs someone to attest what the network saw, which is option 2. What remains available is pricing: the withheld claim needs the same per-name PoW as an open one, bound to its round, and lapses with the same lifetime, so hidden squatting costs what open squatting costs, repeated every period. A short first term for new names raises that recurring cost. Nodes can also refuse to displace a name they have seen settled, but then a node that was offline disagrees with one that was not, trading convergence for stability; that is a policy the owner must pick. Nomen drew the same criticism on Nostr for cheap claims never followed by publication ([Stacker News](https://stacker.news/items/182604)).

**A lottery variant.** OnioNS decides names per period by a verifiable draw over PoW tickets rather than by arrival order ([Victors et al.](https://www.petsymposium.org/popets/2017/popets-2017-0003.pdf)). Here that means: all salted commitments anchored within one epoch compete, and the drand value at the epoch's close picks the winner. It removes network position from in-epoch races and stops an attacker pre-stamping for future epochs, but an agreed ticket set is again what a quorum provides; without one, a withheld ticket from the same epoch still contends. It is highly experimental and was never deployed even in its original form.

**Boxes, cost, risk.** It ticks 1, 2, 3, 5 (best effort on relays), 7, 8, 9 and 10; box 4 partly (names are provisional, and a pre-stamped claim can take one); box 6 partly, depending on how the owner reads it: no coin, fee or wallet, but Bitcoin serves as a public clock and the calendars are a handful of hosts run by very few parties (the notes count four hosts, one of them unverified). Browser cost per claim is one PoW, one BLS check and an OTS proof of a few hundred bytes to a few KB (notes' estimate), plus 80-byte headers from explorers. Relays cannot censor a name without also hiding it from the room, but a relay or a selective publisher can make a claim invisible to part of the room. This construction is the researchers' own; they found no deployed system that uses OTS with a reveal deadline as its ordering rule. **Measure first:** time-to-anchor per calendar from a page, whether the JavaScript OTS client is maintained, calendar rate limits, and BLS verification time in WASM without `simd128`.

### Option 4: a clock-free rule where the heaviest claim wins

This option drops external time anchors for ordering and lets work decide. Each claim and each renewal carries PoW; a name's owner is the key whose chain for that name has the most accumulated work, ties by lowest hash. Nodes converge on any merge with no clock, no anchor and no server, and a settling period in the UI warns that a young name is cheap to overtake. Registration, renewal and update are the shared layer's; expiry still uses drand as the common clock.

It is the simplest thing to build and the weakest on the box the owner most plausibly cares about. A name is never final: whoever out-works the owner at any later time takes it, so first-come becomes "richest ever". The notes calculate that an attacker with 1,000 times the owner's hash rate overtakes a year of the owner's accumulated work in under nine hours (their arithmetic), and the browser-versus-native gap plus rented cores makes such ratios ordinary. An absent owner falls behind by definition, which collides with box 5, and renewals must be full-cost or they become cheap weight. The researchers found no deployment and no paper analysing first-come naming under a highest-work rule. It ticks 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10; boxes 3 and 5 partly; box 4 fails against any funded attacker. Its honest use is as a tiebreak inside another option, or in rooms where nobody would spend a dollar to take a name. **Measure first:** the browser-to-native ratio for the chosen PoW function, since that ratio is the entire security margin.

### Option 5: a registrar key, single or k-of-n, in the room link

The link carries one registrar public key, or n keys with a threshold. A first claim is valid only with the registrar's countersignature; the registrar signs the first valid claim it sees for a name and re-issues a name only after its expiry and grace. It need not be a server: claim requests are stored events on relays, and a registrar that is simply a member's tab works through the queue whenever it is open, so registration is delayed, not blocked. Lookups, updates and renewals never touch the registrar. Two countersigned first claims for one name are proof of misbehaviour. This is GNS's zone model, where `alice` is a delegation under a zone key ([RFC 9498](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9498.html)), and it is what ALFIS's own draft RFC-0001 reaches for: a FROST 5-of-9 emergency key compiled into the binary ([RFC-0001](https://github.com/Revertron/Alfis/blob/master/docs/rfc/0001-emergency-signers.md)).

The key cannot come from the room ID, or every member would hold it, as rooms.md already notes for directories. So it lives in the link, the global room has none, and someone holds it: the room gains an administrator it does not have today. A single key is one point of compromise and of liveness. A threshold signature hides the committee behind one key but needs distributed key generation and resharing with a quorum online at once, which the notes judge the dominant failure for browser tabs. Plain k-of-n countersignatures avoid that but can split their votes between two claims and deadlock a name unless one cosigner sequences and the others attest its order, which is option 2 without the Merkle tree (my analysis). It ticks 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 9; box 8 partly (a holder must appear sometimes); boxes 2 and 10 fail. The master key does buy something: box 4 in full, with no server. It is trivially deployable and the least novel option. **Decide first** whether any party is acceptable in this role; nothing needs measuring.

### Option 6: petnames and per-identity zones, with no room-wide uniqueness

The privacy networks all took this route rather than build consensus. In GNS every identity is a zone, names are resolved by delegation from zones the user chose, and globally unique names are keys ([RFC 9498](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9498.html)); I2P keeps names local, spreads them by address-book subscription, and deliberately gives up global uniqueness ([I2P naming](https://i2p.net/en/docs/overview/naming)); Pkarr's authors say outright that "memorable names need registries, which bring centralization" ([pkarr](https://github.com/pubky/pkarr)).

In a room, each owner key publishes its own signed list of names to the relays; a member's page holds an address book of zones it follows and its own petnames; `web.alice` resolves through the zone the user calls `alice`. Records expire and renew exactly as in the shared layer, and everything is single-writer, so no agreement, clock, PoW or orderer is required, and it works in an empty room and in the global room alike. Two members can bind the same short name to different keys, and a zone a user subscribes to can claim a name before its rightful owner does, which I2P states as the trust a subscription implies. Tor's assessment of GNS applies: choosing a trustworthy zone is difficult, and the choice centralizes ([Tor survey](https://onionservices.torproject.org/research/proposals/usability/discovery/onion-names/)). It ticks 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10; boxes 1 and 4 only within a zone. It is the least risky option and needs nothing new on the wire beyond stored records. **Prototype first:** the address-book and import flow, since usability, not security, decides whether it suffices.

### Option 7: a tiered hybrid that the room link upgrades

The options compose, because they share a record format and differ only in which first claims a node accepts. The hybrid has three tiers. Underneath, always, sit petnames and zones (option 6), which work everywhere. On top, an ownerless room, the global room included, runs time-bracketed claims (option 3) for room-wide names, shown as provisional until settled and with heaviest-work only as the final tiebreak. And a room whose link names cosigner keys, or a log with witnesses, accepts only countersigned first claims (options 5 or 2), getting hard first-come in exchange for naming a party. The resolver marks which tier answered.

This mirrors how rooms already treat infrastructure: defaults that are best-effort and public, and "a serious room runs its own Nostr relay and derper and puts them in its link" ([rooms.md](../docs/networking/rooms.md)). Its boxes are those of the active tier. Its costs are the union of what it includes: more code paths and more to explain to a user, and one new question, whether a room's naming policy is bound into the room ID or only into the link. If only into the link, two members holding links with different cosigners see different registries for the same room, as two members with disjoint relay lists already cannot find each other; binding the policy into the ID avoids that but means an upgraded room is a new room. No deployed system combines these tiers, so the novelty risk is option 3's plus integration.

## The hybrid first, then four measurements and eleven decisions

**Recommendation.** Choose option 7, built in three steps that each deliver something usable. First, the shared layer with petnames and zones: records, per-name chains, relay parking, reconciliation, the local replica and the resolver change. It is common to every option, so no work is wasted whatever the owner later decides, and it gives readable names at once. Second, time-bracketed claims as the room-wide arbiter for ownerless rooms, with names marked provisional until settled. Third, link-declared cosigners for rooms that want hard first-come, starting with plain countersignatures and moving to a witnessed log only if a room operator wants one.

The case for this over the witnessed log, which the consensus research ranks first on security, is the project's defining constraint: there is no backend, and the persistence research found no public log or witness service a page can use today. Recommending option 2 alone would leave the global room and every casual room without names. The case against the hybrid deserves equal weight: its default tier does not deliver box 4. A name in an ownerless room can be lost to a claim stamped earlier and held back, and no amount of engineering closes that without a witness; PoW only prices it. If the owner reads box 4 as non-negotiable, the choice is between option 5 (accept a key, no server) and option 2 (accept a server, weaker key), and the hybrid's default tier should be dropped to petnames only. I would not pick option 1 or option 4 as the arbiter: the first imports a liveness failure that its own upstream is still repairing into a population less stable than ALFIS's, and the second fails box 4 outright.

**What to measure before the spec.** The notes leave four numbers unknown that decide parameters or feasibility. A retention canary: publish registry-shaped events of an unassigned kind to the six default relays and re-read them weekly, since retention over weeks is the whole of box 5 in an empty room. PoW asymmetry: the same Argon2id parameters in Chromium WASM and natively on one machine, because the existing figures come from an old 1 MiB Argon2d table and unsourced cloud prices. Bootstrap CPU: BIP-340 verification, single and batched, in this toolchain's WASM, to confirm or replace the assumed 15–40 s for 100k records. And the anchor path: OpenTimestamps time-to-anchor, calendar limits and client health from a page, plus one BLS12-381 verification in WASM. Smaller checks follow: Negentropy bytes at 100k with small diffs, relays' bulk-write limits beyond the 60-event burst, and Blossom servers' CORS and retention if snapshots are wanted.

**Decisions only the owner can make.** The first is whether provisional names are acceptable in ownerless rooms, or whether box 4 outranks boxes 8 and 10. The second is whether a registry may store events on relays and act as a directory, which reverses two binding rooms decisions. The third is whether using Bitcoin as a clock through OpenTimestamps, with no coin, fee or wallet, counts as a tie to cryptocurrency under box 6; if it does, option 3 loses its not-after bound and collapses toward option 4. The fourth is the owner identity: a separate BIP-340 key per identity or per name, or XEdDSA on the node key, and with it whether persistent keys visible to relays are acceptable. The fifth is new dependencies, each needing an OK: a BLS pairing library for drand, an Argon2 implementation, a Negentropy implementation. The sixth is the global room's policy, and whether the page may ever ship default cosigners, which would make the page's author an authority. The seventh is whether naming policy binds into the room ID or only the link. The eighth is live propagation: a shared relay tag only, or member gossip, which reveals who is online. The ninth is the lifecycle: lifetime, grace, semi-expiry, how early renewal opens, and the PoW budget a tab may spend beside an emulator. The tenth is whether private rooms seal their records from relays. The eleventh is the settled-name policy in option 3: always follow the deterministic rule and accept late reveals, or pin settled names locally and accept that nodes can disagree.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question the owner is answering. It began as "which distributed ledger fits a browser" and ends as "which party, if any, may say that a claim was public at a given time". Ledgers, DAGs, sleepy consensus and VDFs all turn out to rest on a coin, a roster or hardware a tab lacks; the only coinless chain in production orders by a handful of signer keys. What is left is a small, proven core of self-certifying records that needs no agreement at all, and one narrow gap where agreement is unavoidable.

That gap can be filled by a server, by a key, by a public clock that leaves names provisional, or by not having room-wide names. Because the core is shared, the choice is reversible in a way architectural choices rarely are: a room can start with petnames and provisional names and add cosigners later without changing a record. The decision that cannot be deferred is the first one on the list above, whether a name that is probably yours is good enough.
